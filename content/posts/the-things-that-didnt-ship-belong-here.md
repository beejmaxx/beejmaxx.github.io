A conventional portfolio is a room after the cleaning crew has left. The cables are hidden. The failed prototypes are gone. Every project has a tidy problem, a tidy process, and a result that appears inevitable in retrospect.

That is useful for scanning. It is not very useful for understanding how someone works.

I want this site to keep a different kind of record: the shipped things **and** the attempts that changed what came next.

## Failure needs an edit

Showing every abandoned directory would be noise. Turning every wrong turn into a heroic origin story would be another kind of fiction. The useful unit is a short, specific postmortem:

1. What was I trying to prove?
2. Which assumption broke first?
3. What evidence did I ignore or discover late?
4. What moved into the next project?

That last question matters. A dead project can still produce a durable pattern, a sharper instinct, or ten lines of code that survive for years.

> The point is not to celebrate failure. It is to stop throwing away the evidence.

## Status is part of the story

Projects in this archive will not all be labeled “complete.” Some will be experiments, pauses, forks, useful failures, or work that simply belongs to an earlier version of the web.

Those labels are not grades. They are context.

When I write about a failed project, I will try to be concrete enough that the lesson can be challenged. “It did not work” says nothing. “The setup cost was larger than the problem” says something a future project can use.

## The portfolio as a lab notebook

This is the model I like: a lab notebook with a good index. The front page can still show the clearest work. The archive keeps the whole lineage. The notes connect decisions across time.

The result should be more interesting than a highlight reel because it contains motion—ideas being tested, discarded, rediscovered, and occasionally shipped.

So the failures stay. Not all of them, and not without an edit. But enough to make the finished work honest.
